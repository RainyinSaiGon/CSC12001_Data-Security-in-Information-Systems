\section{Introduction and Scope of Research}

Malicious software, commonly referred to as malware, represents one of the most persistent and evolving threats in the digital landscape.
As defined in information security literature, malware is any program or set of instructions designed to perform unauthorized operations on a system without the user's consent \cite{ref1}.
From early ``sneakernet'' transmissions via floppy disks to today's advanced, multi-stage, and stealth-enabled campaigns,
malware has evolved from an academic curiosity into a mature criminal and geopolitical instrument.

To evaluate this threat rigorously, malware impact should be examined through core security principles and operational risk lenses.

\subsection{Alignment with the CIA Triad}

To fully comprehend the danger posed by malware, its consequences must be mapped to the CIA Triad (Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability):

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Confidentiality:} Malware such as spyware, keyloggers, and credential-stealing trojans is engineered to covertly exfiltrate sensitive information, including login credentials, personal records, and intellectual property.
	\item \textbf{Integrity:} Viruses and worms can alter system files, inject malicious instructions into legitimate applications, and corrupt data flows, thereby undermining trust in information correctness and system behavior \cite{ref1}.
	\item \textbf{Availability:} Ransomware encrypts critical data while botnets launch distributed denial-of-service campaigns; both tactics degrade or completely disrupt service availability.
\end{itemize}

Using the CIA Triad as an analytical frame clarifies how malware transforms technical compromise into business, legal, and societal impact.

\subsection{Scope of Research}

This report treats malware as a full lifecycle problem, from origin and propagation to detection, response, and long-term resilience.
The scope of research is organized into the following domains:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Classification and Architecture:} analysis of internal behavior and propagation models across malware families, including viruses, worms, trojan horses, logic bombs, spyware, adware, botnets, keystroke loggers, and malicious browser extensions.
	\item \textbf{Vulnerabilities and Enablers:} examination of technical and operational conditions that allow malware execution, with emphasis on active content abuse, homepage hijacking, and web server compromise patterns.
	\item \textbf{Attack Vectors and Methodology:} discussion of attacker entry pathways, including social engineering, spam delivery, software vulnerability exploitation, and DoS/DDoS operations.
	\item \textbf{Anatomy of an Attack:} attacker motivation models, objective categories, structured vs. unstructured attack styles, and phased execution from reconnaissance to trace-covering.
	\item \textbf{Future Trends:} projection of evolving threats such as AI-assisted malware, fileless techniques, IoT botnets, triple-extortion ransomware, and software supply-chain compromise.
	\item \textbf{Implications for Defense:} synthesis of lessons for layered protection, incident readiness, and cyber resilience in modern organizations.
\end{enumerate}

Together, these sections provide a structured foundation for understanding malware not merely as malicious code, but as a socio-technical threat ecosystem requiring layered and continuously adaptive defense.
